Epigraph
The confession from hell

From Dante's Inferno, Canto XXVII. Guido da Montefeltro speaks from his flame, believing no one can carry his words back to the living world.

Read the epigraph
S'io credesse che mia risposta fosse A persona che mai tornasse al mondo, Questa fiamma staria senza piu scosse. Ma perciocche giammai di questo fondo Non torno vivo alcun, s'i'odo il vero, Senza tema d'infamia ti rispondo.
Guido speaks freely only because he believes no one will ever hear. This frames the entire poem: Prufrock's confession is possible only because he assumes it will go unheard. The world of "Prufrock" mirrors Dante's Hell, a place of stasis, where nothing changes and no one escapes.
I. The invitation
Let us go then, you and I

The poem opens with the promise of movement, but immediately undercuts it with the most disorienting simile in modern poetry.

Read this passage
Let us go then, you and I, When the evening is spread out against the sky Like a patient etherized upon a table; Let us go, through certain half-deserted streets, The muttering retreats Of restless nights in one-night cheap hotels And sawdust restaurants with oyster-shells: Streets that follow like a tedious argument Of insidious intent To lead you to an overwhelming question ... Oh, do not ask, "What is it?" Let us go and make our visit. In the room the women come and go Talking of Michelangelo.
The etherized patient is the poem's first shock: beauty rendered clinical. And notice how the "overwhelming question" is immediately deflected: "Oh, do not ask." This evasion is Prufrock's defining gesture. He will spend the entire poem circling without arriving.
II. The fog
The yellow smoke that rubs its muzzle

The fog becomes a cat, one of the poem's most celebrated transformations. The city itself is alive, languid, and indifferent.

Read this passage
The yellow fog that rubs its back upon the window-panes, The yellow smoke that rubs its muzzle on the window-panes, Licked its tongue into the corners of the evening, Lingered upon the pools that stand in drains, Let fall upon its back the soot that falls from chimneys, Slipped by the terrace, made a sudden leap, And seeing that it was a soft October night, Curled once about the house, and fell asleep.
This stanza is a single, sinuous sentence. The fog-as-cat never states its metaphor outright: it simply enacts it. Everything rubs, licks, lingers, curls, sleeps. The city breathes while Prufrock watches, paralyzed. Note how the entire stanza achieves what Prufrock cannot: unselfconscious action.
III. The paralysis
Do I dare disturb the universe?

The poem's emotional center. Prufrock catalogues his inadequacies with excruciating precision, measuring his life in coffee spoons.

Read this passage
And indeed there will be time To wonder, "Do I dare?" and, "Do I dare?" Time to turn back and descend the stair, With a bald spot in the middle of my hair, (They will say: "How his hair is growing thin!") My morning coat, my collar mounting firmly to the chin, My necktie rich and modest, but asserted by a simple pin, (They will say: "But how his arms and legs are thin!") Do I dare Disturb the universe? In a minute there is time For decisions and revisions which a minute will reverse. For I have known them all already, known them all: Have known the evenings, mornings, afternoons, I have measured out my life with coffee spoons; I know the voices dying with a dying fall Beneath the music from a farther room. So how should I presume?
"Do I dare / Disturb the universe?": the grandest possible question, asked by a man worried about his bald spot. That collision of scale is pure Eliot. And "I have measured out my life with coffee spoons" may be the single most devastating line in modern poetry: an entire existence reduced to polite, identical increments.
IV. The desire
Arms that are braceleted and white and bare

Prufrock's longing surfaces, and is immediately deflected into abstraction and self-mockery.

Read this passage
And I have known the arms already, known them all, Arms that are braceleted and white and bare (But in the lamplight, downed with light brown hair!) Is it perfume from a dress That makes me so digress? Arms that lie along a table, or wrap about a shawl. And should I then presume? And how should I begin?
The parenthetical, "(But in the lamplight, downed with light brown hair!)", is the poem's most intimate moment. That exclamation mark is almost involuntary, a gasp of sensual attention he cannot suppress. And then immediately: "Is it perfume from a dress / That makes me so digress?" He catches himself feeling and retreats into wordplay.
V. The crisis
I was afraid

The moment of truth, which never arrives. Prufrock sees the eternal Footman, and confesses the simplest, most devastating thing.

Read this passage
Should I, after tea and cakes and ices, Have the strength to force the moment to its crisis? But though I have wept and fasted, wept and prayed, Though I have seen my head (grown slightly bald) brought in upon a platter, I am no prophet, and here's no great matter; I have seen the moment of my greatness flicker, And I have seen the eternal Footman hold my coat, and snicker, And in short, I was afraid.
"And in short, I was afraid." After all the elaborate evasions, the literary allusions, the self-deprecating wit: four plain words. This is the poem's confession. Everything else has been decoration around this core admission. The eternal Footman (Death, judgment, the gaze of others) does not threaten. He snickers. The indignity is worse than the danger.
VI. The diminishment
I am not Prince Hamlet

Prufrock compares himself to Shakespeare's great indecisive prince, only to demote himself further. He is not even the lead in his own tragedy.

Read this passage
No! I am not Prince Hamlet, nor was meant to be; Am an attendant lord, one that will do To swell a progress, start a scene or two, Advise the prince; no doubt, an easy tool, Deferential, glad to be of use, Politic, cautious, and meticulous; Full of high sentence, but a bit obtuse; At times, indeed, almost ridiculous, Almost, at times, the Fool. I grow old ... I grow old ... I shall wear the bottoms of my trousers rolled. Shall I part my hair behind? Do I dare to eat a peach? I shall wear white flannel trousers, and walk upon the beach.
The genius here is "almost ridiculous, / Almost, at times, the Fool." Not even fully ridiculous. Not even fully the Fool. He cannot commit to anything, not even self-abasement. And "Do I dare to eat a peach?", after "Do I dare / Disturb the universe?", the question has shrunk from cosmic to absurd. But for Prufrock, both are equally impossible.
VII. The drowning
Till human voices wake us, and we drown

The poem's devastating final movement. Fantasy, beauty, exclusion, and the killing return to reality.

Read this passage
I have heard the mermaids singing, each to each. I do not think that they will sing to me. I have seen them riding seaward on the waves Combing the white hair of the waves blown back When the wind blows the water white and black. We have lingered in the chambers of the sea By sea-girls wreathed with seaweed red and brown Till human voices wake us, and we drown.
The mermaids sing, but not to Prufrock. Even in fantasy, he cannot imagine being worthy of attention. And then the final three lines shift to "we": suddenly, terribly, this is not just his problem. We have all lingered in the chambers of the sea. And when the human voices come (the real world, the social world, the world that demands action), we do not rise. We drown. The ordinary is more lethal than the mythic.
